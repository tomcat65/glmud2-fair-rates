First — what exactly did the district propose?

At the August 3, 2026 board meeting the district presented two rate scenarios, alongside its case for raising rates at all: the May 2026 request for new bond authorization did not pass, sales-tax (SPA) revenue collapsed, and on current rates its reserves go deeply negative over the next decade. Some previously approved bond authorization may remain available, but that status is unconfirmed pending the district's Notice to Purchaser or bond records. The revenue need is real — this page disputes the shape, not the amount. Here is each scenario, as presented (verify in the original handouts, downloadable below):

SCENARIO 1 — the board's apparent lean

Water: base $25 → $30, and it now includes only 5,000 gal (today's base covers 10,000). Above that, new tiers from $1.50 up to $5.00 per 1,000 gal.

"Sewer": flat charge $20 → $55–$75 depending on usage — and per the handout's own note, this increase "is designed to cover Garbage & Recycling."

Projected revenue: $950,922/yr — exactly the FY27 budget's water+wastewater line. The strategy itself suggests a second phase of increases in 2028.

The cap is real — and it is on the tax lever

The records now show three funding doors. Bonds require voter authorization. Operation-and-maintenance tax growth has a voter mechanism under Texas Water Code, depending on the district's legal classification. Fees are the route presented scenarios use without a comparable voter-election cap found in the materials reviewed.

THREE FUNDING DOORS

Bonds: new authorization requires voter approval; the May 2026 request did not pass. Some previously approved authorization may remain available, but that is unconfirmed pending MUD 2's Notice to Purchaser or bond records.

O&M tax growth: for a developed district, Water Code §49.23602 requires an automatic election when the adopted rate would raise taxes on the average homestead by more than 3.5%. If MUD 2 is not a developed district, §49.23603 describes an 8% threshold at which voters may require an election by petition.

Fees: Water Code §49.212 authorizes district charges for facilities or services. No comparable voter-election cap on fees has been found in the reviewed materials; a possible contractual cap remains open in the pending records request.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR S3

S3's +4.5¢ component is about 35% growth on the average-homestead tax used in the brief — roughly ten times the 3.5% trigger and above the 8% alternative. It cannot simply be adopted by the board without the applicable voter mechanism. The WCID notice proves WCID's regime, not MUD 2's classification; the Truth-in-Taxation worksheet should settle that question.

THE FAIR REFRAME

Fee-heavy scenarios are not a conspiracy: they are the legal, rational, currently unlocked door. But that also means they route around the mechanism that asks the community first. The honest comparison is not just tax versus fee — it is tax-and-ballot versus fee-without-a-ballot.

That is not a defect discovered late — it is the design, now made explicit: the honest version of the two-levers choice is a ballot. A district that trusts its numbers should have no fear of that vote.

A bond and a rate plan answer different questions

Tim Fleming, a neighbor, asked for a plain-language explanation of the bond discussion. The key distinction is authorization versus collection: a bond can authorize capital borrowing, while an operating rate plan funds recurring service.

AUTHORIZATION IS A LIMIT, NOT A PURCHASE

A bond authorization is like a credit-card limit: it permits borrowing up to an amount, but it is not itself a purchase. Debt can be issued in series as projects are ready, subject to the district's legal and financial process.

WHY A BOND CAN FIT CAPITAL

A 30-year asset can be paid for over the period in which residents use it. Under pay-as-you-go, today's residents fund the full repair and may move before receiving the full benefit. With a bond, a resident who leaves stops paying and a new owner picks up the bond tax for the years they use the asset. Pay-as-you-go avoids interest, but it asks current ratepayers to carry the full cost up front. The district presentation recommended capital base fees “in lieu of long-term bonds” after the May 2026 request for new bond authorization did not pass. That does not establish that all prior authorization is exhausted.

THE HONEST TRADEOFF

Bond debt service is paid through property taxes, which scale with taxable value. Interest and a long commitment are the counterweight. Tomas's position is that an S3 operating design and a carefully described capital plan can be complementary, not substitutes.

ONE REAL DIFFERENCE: DEDUCTIBILITY

Bond debt service arrives as a property tax. A qualifying taxpayer who itemizes may be able to deduct state and local property taxes, subject to the federal SALT limit; water, sewer, and trash service charges generally are not deductible as itemized taxes. For 2026, the IRS states that the overall SALT limit is $40,400 and is reduced above $505,000 of modified AGI, but not below $10,000. Under current law, the higher limit is scheduled to return to $10,000 in 2030. See IRS guidance ↗ See the current-law schedule ↗

This is not a benefit everyone receives: households using the standard deduction get no separate itemized SALT deduction, and Tomas says he does not itemize. The fairness case for the tax channel rests mainly on value-scaling; deductibility is a limited bonus where it applies.

Common questions

Why is the district raising rates at all?

The district's stated case: the May 2026 request for new bond authorization did not pass, sales-tax (SPA) revenue collapsed, and on current rates its reserves go deeply negative over the next decade. Nobody here disputes that the district needs the money it says it needs — the brief verifies the $950,923 revenue target against the FY2027 budget line by line. The disagreement is about the shape: who carries it, and whether prices reflect measured costs.

Am I in Grand Lakes MUD 2?

Look at the top of your water bill. If it says GRAND LAKES MUD 2, this is your district (neighbors on adjacent streets may be in MUD 1, MUD 4, or the WCID — their rates are set separately).

What are Scenario 1 and Scenario 2?

Scenario 1: water base rises to $30 (includes 5,000 gal) with new tiers, and the flat sewer charge rises from $20 to $55–75 depending on usage. Scenario 2: a steeper water structure, volumetric sewer, plus a flat $100-per-connection monthly "Capital Project Base Fee" — identical for every home regardless of size, value, or usage. The board appeared to lean toward Scenario 1, with a second phase suggested for 2028.

What is the counter-proposal (S3)?

A structure built from measured costs that raises the same total revenue within 0.2%: a low $15 water base, marginal water priced at or above its true $6.95 cost, sewer near its actual $47 connection cost, garbage on its own honest line, commercial rates 25% above Scenario 1, and a modest 4.5¢ tax component — the one lever the presented study never modeled. That tax component is a voter question, not a board-only adjustment: it would exceed the applicable Water Code tax-growth threshold. Every formula is open in the workbook below.

Do I pay more than one district tax?

Likely yes, if your property is in both Grand Lakes MUD 2 and Grand Lakes WCID. They levy separately and are not included on the county bill. WCID is functionally a drainage/stormwater district in the reviewed budget, not a second MUD 2 water utility. Check the Wheeler statement for MUD 2 and the Bonnerjee/B&A statement for WCID, or ask your escrow provider. Confirm the district names on your own statements.

Doesn't the tax component just move my cost to a different bill?

For this verified home, yes — deliberately: S3's total household cost is $198.93/month versus $217.04 under Scenario 1. The difference is distribution: a tax scales with taxable value, a flat fee ignores it. Below roughly $674,000 of taxable value, the flat-fee version charges more than the value-scaled version would. The study's own footnote prices the lever (each 1¢ ≈ $45,732/yr); it was simply never modeled.

Is there a cap on the rate increase?

A cap is confirmed on the tax side, not on water rates: Water Code §49.23602 uses an automatic-election path above 3.5% on the average homestead for a developed district; §49.23603 provides an 8% petition-election path for a district that is not in the other categories. The WCID notice confirms WCID's path, not MUD 2's classification. No statutory cap on water rates has been found in the reviewed materials; whether a contractual cap exists in the district's Houston consent agreement is the subject of a pending records request.

What can I actually do?

Three things. Read the brief (below) so you know the numbers. Check your own bill with the calculator above. And come to the next board meeting — the board accepts resident comments, and rate design decisions are made there. The ask is modest: publish the cost-of-service measurement and show the community both levers — fees and taxes — side by side, before anything is adopted.
