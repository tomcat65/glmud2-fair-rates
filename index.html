Next—what exactly did the district propose?

At the August 3, 2026 board meeting, the district presented two options for increasing monthly water bills.

Why an increase? The district says it needs substantially more revenue. Sales-tax (SPA) revenue has fallen sharply, voters did not approve the district's May 2026 request for permission to issue new bonds, and the district projects that its reserves could run out and fall below zero over the next decade if nothing changes.

In other words, the district does need more money. The main question is how that cost should be divided among customers and taxpayers.

Scenario 1 includes an important assumption. It counts on $3.3 million in borrowing permission that voters approved in an earlier election and that may still be available without another vote. The district has not yet confirmed whether that permission remains. Its official property disclosure, called the Notice to Purchaser, or its bond records should answer that question.

This page does not dispute the need for additional revenue. It asks whether the proposed rates divide the cost fairly.

When voters get a say

The records show three ways the district could collect the money. Voters must approve permission to issue new bonds. A large increase in the district's operating property tax — often called the O&M tax — can also lead to an election or petition process. The exact rule depends on how MUD 2 is legally classified under Texas law. The materials reviewed show no similar voter process for water-bill fees.

THREE WAYS TO COLLECT THE MONEY

Bonds: voters must approve permission to issue new bonds. The May 2026 request did not pass. Scenario 1 nevertheless counts on $3.3 million in borrowing permission that voters approved in an earlier election and that may still be available without another vote. MUD 2's Notice to Purchaser or bond records should confirm whether that permission remains.

The district's operating property tax (O&M tax): if MUD 2 is legally classified as a developed district, Water Code §49.23602 requires an automatic election when the adopted rate would raise taxes on the average home by more than 3.5%. If MUD 2 falls into another classification, §49.23603 describes an 8% threshold at which voters may call an election by petition. These limits apply to the operating tax, not to property taxes used to repay voter-approved bond principal and interest. A neighboring WCID document explains that WCID may tax as needed to repay voter-approved bonds. That document tells us how WCID works, but it does not by itself establish what MUD 2 is legally allowed to do.

Fees: Water Code §49.212 authorizes district charges for facilities or services. No comparable voter-election cap on fees has been found in the reviewed materials; a possible contractual cap remains open in the pending records request.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR S3

S3's proposed 4.5¢ property-tax increase would raise the operating tax on the average home by about 35% — roughly ten times the 3.5% trigger and above the alternative 8% threshold. The board could not simply adopt it without the election or petition process that applies to MUD 2. The neighboring WCID document does not settle MUD 2's classification. The district's official tax-rate calculation worksheet, called the Truth-in-Taxation worksheet, should answer that question.

WHY FEES MAY BE EASIER TO ADOPT

The fee-heavy options are not a conspiracy. Based on the materials reviewed, fees are simply the only option that does not involve voters. A new bond would require voter approval first. After approval, the district would use property taxes to repay the bond's principal and interest; those repayment taxes are not limited by the operating-tax growth rule. That is why borrowing for long-term projects remains a reasonable option to discuss even though MUD 2's remaining permission to borrow has not been confirmed.

The plain-language conclusion: a large operating-tax increase or permission to issue new bonds would give voters a role; a water-bill fee would not. The operating-tax limits do not mean bonds are unavailable. They mean the community should clearly distinguish an operating-tax increase from property taxes used to repay voter-approved bonds.

Bonds and monthly rates pay for different needs

A concerned neighbor asked for a plain-language explanation of the bond discussion. Voter approval gives the district permission to borrow for major, long-term projects. Monthly water rates pay for recurring services and can also pay directly for repairs.

VOTER APPROVAL SETS A BORROWING LIMIT

Voter-approved borrowing permission works somewhat like a credit-card limit. Voters allow the district to borrow up to a stated amount, but that approval does not mean all the money is borrowed immediately. The district can borrow in stages as projects become ready, subject to its legal and financial process.

WHY BORROWING CAN FIT LONG-LIVED PROJECTS

A repair or replacement that lasts 30 years can be paid for over the years residents use it. Paying entirely from current rates avoids interest, but today's residents carry the full cost up front and may move before receiving the full benefit. Borrowing spreads the cost, with future owners continuing to repay it while they use the system. Fully paying for the same project list from current rates would cost the verified home an estimated $190.89 per month. Paying shorter-cycle repairs from current rates and financing long-lived replacements would cost an estimated $117.70 per month. The district presentation recommended project fees instead of long-term bonds after voters did not approve the May 2026 request. Scenario 1 still assumes $3.3 million of earlier voter-approved permission to borrow remains. District records need to confirm that assumption.

THE HONEST TRADEOFF

Property taxes repay bond principal and interest, so owners of higher-value property generally pay more. The reviewed Notice to Purchaser says the neighboring WCID may tax as needed to repay voter-approved bonds. That document tells us how the neighboring WCID works, but it does not by itself establish what MUD 2 is legally allowed to do. Borrowing adds interest and a long repayment period; in return, voters approve it first and the cost can be spread across the years the projects are used. A fair monthly-rate plan and a clearly explained plan for major projects can work together rather than replace one another.

ONE REAL DIFFERENCE: DEDUCTIBILITY

Property taxes used to repay bonds may be deductible for a qualifying taxpayer who itemizes deductions. Water, sewer, and trash service charges generally are not deductible as itemized taxes. For 2026, the IRS states that the overall limit on state and local tax deductions is $40,400. That limit is reduced above $505,000 of modified adjusted gross income, but not below $10,000. Under current law, the higher limit is scheduled to return to $10,000 in 2030. See IRS guidance ↗ See the current-law schedule ↗

This is not a benefit everyone receives: households using the standard deduction get no separate itemized SALT deduction, and Tomas says he does not itemize. The fairness case for the tax channel rests mainly on value-scaling; deductibility is a limited bonus where it applies.

What I'd choose, and why — you decide for yourself

Let me say the hard part first, because you deserve to hear it plainly: there is no option where your bill stays where it is. The district needs more money — that part checks out, line by line, against its own budget. The only real question left is which version of the increase we pick. And we do get a say in that.

Here's how the choices land on a typical Grand Lakes home — about 12,000 gallons a month, taxable value around $355,000. Every number below comes from the district's own documents, downloadable on this page, so don't take my word for anything.

The board's likely plan (Scenario 1) puts that home at about $216 a month. It hides the trash bill inside a line called "sewer," and the families who conserve the most get the biggest percentage increases. The plan also comes with a second round of increases already suggested for 2028.

Scenario 3 (S3) is not one of the district's options — it's mine. The district presented two scenarios on August 3; this is a neighbor's alternative, built from the district's own numbers, and the board would have to choose to take it up. I call it Scenario 3 because it belongs in the same comparison: it raises the district the same money and puts the same home at about $199. The difference is just honesty in the shape: you pay for the water you actually use, sewer costs what sewer costs, and trash gets its own line on the bill. If your sprinkler system is wasting money, your bill shows you — and you can fix it. Under a flat fee, there's nothing to fix. Mine goes up the most on my street under Scenario 3, because my sprinklers were the biggest wasters. I proposed it anyway.

Then there's the repair plan — and this one isn't anyone's fault. Our plant was built around 2001, equipment lasts 15 to 25 years, and the district's own list totals $15.6 million over ten years. The proposed $100 monthly fee covers about half of that, and the district's study already recommends raising it yearly. So the $100 isn't a price. It's a starting bid.

The alternative is the one we already voted on once: bonds. Borrow for the equipment that lasts 20 to 50 years, repaid through property taxes — about $41 a month on that typical home instead of $100, and it funds the whole plan. I understand why the May bond vote failed: nobody showed us what the money bought or what it cost our own house per month. That's what this page is for. Look at the list. Do the math on your own home with the calculator above.

One more thing an engineer owes you: the $15.6 million is a planning estimate — the district's own document calls it "a budgetary tool" to be reviewed annually — and planning estimates carry cushions. Roughly a quarter of that total is contingency and engineering allowance, not equipment. That's normal and prudent at this stage. But it is also the strongest reason to prefer bonds over a fixed fee: bonds are only issued when projects are actually bid, so if real prices come in lower, we borrow less and the tax is smaller. A flat fee written into the rate order keeps collecting the cushion forever, whether it was needed or not. You should never lock a permanent rate or tax against a planning-stage estimate — you lock it against real bids.

Which is my last ask, and it costs the district almost nothing: before adopting any permanent fee or tax for the repair plan, take the first two years of projects to bid-ready estimates and publish them. The near-term list is already defined — the tank recoating, the treatment-plant electrical work, the lift station rehabilitation. Real quotes on real scopes. Then size whatever we adopt against those numbers, and true it up in public once a year, exactly as the plan's own notes say it should be reviewed. Good estimates make good neighbors.

Add it up: Scenario 3 plus the bond costs that home about $240 a month with everything actually funded — and treat that as the ceiling, not the bill, because it is priced at the plan's full face value before a single project is competitively bid. Against it: $216 that funds none of the repairs and grows again in 2028. I'd rather pay $24 more and stop getting surprised.

That's my conclusion. It doesn't have to be yours. Every document is on this page, the workbook has every formula open, and if you run the numbers and land somewhere different, bring it to the next board meeting — that's exactly where this decision gets made, and the room works better full.

Common questions

Why is the district raising rates at all?

The district says it needs more money because sales-tax (SPA) revenue has fallen sharply, voters did not approve its May 2026 request for permission to issue new bonds, and its projections show reserves could run out and fall below zero over the next decade. This page does not dispute the need for additional revenue. The brief checks the $950,923 target against the FY2027 budget line by line. The disagreement is about how the cost should be divided and whether the proposed prices reflect measured costs.

What will the $100 Capital Base Fee actually fund?

About half. The “Capital Project Base Fee” is a flat $100 monthly fee on every home/account. It would raise $818,400 a year, while the district's ten-year plan for major repairs and infrastructure projects averages $1,562,207 a year. Over ten years, the gap is roughly $7.4 million. The rate study recommends further annual fee increases if a bond election does not succeed. The complete plan, analysis, and source response are downloadable above.

What major repairs and infrastructure projects are planned?

The wastewater plant and lift stations are reaching the end of their expected first service lives. Most of the plant dates to 2001, and most lift stations date to 2000–2006, with expected service lives of 15–25 years. Planned work includes generators, electrical equipment, tank recoating, pumps, and piping. This is not a surprise or an accusation of mismanagement; infrastructure eventually needs repair or replacement. The question is which payment method should cover it.

Am I in Grand Lakes MUD 2?

Look at the top of your water bill. If it says GRAND LAKES MUD 2, this is your district (neighbors on adjacent streets may be in MUD 1, MUD 4, or the WCID — their rates are set separately).

What are Scenario 1 and Scenario 2?

Scenario 1: the water minimum rises to $30 and includes 5,000 gallons, with higher prices as use increases. The flat sewer charge rises from $20 to $55–75 depending on use. Scenario 2: the water price rises more steeply, the sewer charge increases with use, and every home/account pays a flat $100 monthly “Capital Project Base Fee” to help fund major projects. The board appeared to favor Scenario 1, with a second round of increases suggested for 2028.

What is the counter-proposal (S3)?

S3 is built from measured costs and raises the same total amount of money within 0.2%. It uses a low $15 water minimum, charges at least the district's $6.95 cost for additional water, sets sewer near its measured $47 cost per billed account, shows garbage as a separate line, charges commercial accounts 25% more than Scenario 1, and adds 4.5¢ to the property-tax rate. Voters would get a say on that tax increase because it exceeds the applicable Texas threshold. Every formula is available in the workbook below.

Do I pay more than one district tax?

Likely yes, if your property is in both Grand Lakes MUD 2 and Grand Lakes WCID. They levy separately and are not included on the county bill. WCID is functionally a drainage/stormwater district in the reviewed budget, not a second MUD 2 water utility. Check the Wheeler statement for MUD 2 and the Bonnerjee/B&A statement for WCID, or ask your escrow provider. Confirm the district names on your own statements.

Doesn't the tax component just move my cost to a different bill?

For this verified home, yes — deliberately. S3's total household cost is $198.93 per month versus $216.04 under Scenario 1. The difference is how the cost is divided: property tax increases with taxable value, while a flat fee does not. Below roughly $674,000 in taxable value, the flat-fee option costs more than the property-tax option would. The study's own footnote says each 1¢ of tax raises about $45,732 per year, but it did not model that option.

Is there a cap on the rate increase?

A limit is confirmed for increases in the district's operating property tax, not for water rates. For a district legally classified as developed, Water Code §49.23602 requires an automatic election above a 3.5% increase on the average home. Section 49.23603 provides an 8% threshold at which voters in some other districts may call an election by petition. A neighboring WCID document explains WCID's rule, but it does not establish MUD 2's classification. The district's official tax-rate calculation worksheet should answer that question. No state-law cap on MUD 2 water rates has been found in the materials reviewed. A pending records request asks whether the district's agreement with Houston contains a separate contractual cap.

What can I actually do?

Three things. Read the brief below so you know the numbers. Check your own bill with the calculator above. And come to the next board meeting — the board accepts resident comments, and rate decisions are made there. The request is straightforward: publish the measured cost of service and show the community both the flat-fee and property-tax options before adopting one.
